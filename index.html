00 / Statement Artist Statement

A rule, iterated without end, becomes a field with its own life.

I am an Iranian-born artist working at the intersection of cellular automata and generative systems. My work begins with a simple observation: the local rule — repeated over and over on a grid — has always been a form of computation, long before computers.

Game of Seeds renders a cellular automaton in the tradition of Conway's Game of Life, but with a custom rule: a dead cell comes alive if exactly two of its eight neighbours are alive, and a live cell dies otherwise. The grid is seeded with 10–30 three-cell groups placed at random positions and orientations, then iterated between 30 and 2030 times.

Every live cell is drawn as a coloured dot with a soft glow, at a scale proportional to the cell size. Every dead cell is drawn as a black dot, so the pattern reads as an ornamental mosaic against the coloured ground. The result sits between biological simulation and geometric ornament. Each piece is defined by a numeric seed and can be regenerated, documented, and adapted for print, textile, and surface applications.

On AI tools — where I encountered technical obstacles, I turned to AI tools for debugging and code optimization. Every structural, aesthetic, and conceptual decision remained my own.

03 / Process

Structure
behind the rule.

01 / Rule

Neighbour Sum

Every cell checks the sum of its eight neighbours. If the cell is dead and the sum equals 2, it comes alive. Otherwise it stays dead, or dies if it was alive. That single rule produces branching, spiralling, cellular, and crystalline patterns depending on the starting seed.

02 / Seed

Three-Cell Groups

The grid starts with 10–30 three-cell groups. Each group is placed either horizontally or vertically, at a random position within the grid, with a check that no two groups overlap. This initial condition determines the entire subsequent evolution of the field.

03 / Iterate

30 to 2030 Steps

The rule is applied 30–2030 times, replacing the grid with its successor at every step. The number of iterations controls how far the pattern has evolved — early steps are sparse and structured, late steps fill the field with dense clusters and cellular detail.
